About Me

I am a Research Group Leader in the Social Foundations of Computation department at the Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems in Tübingen, Germany, hosted by Moritz Hardt.

I build the theoretical foundations for incentive-aware evaluation. I study AI evaluation and algorithmic decision-making systems in which people, models, and institutions interact strategically. Currently, I am particularly interested in LLM evaluation ecosystems and in incentives in scientific discovery.

I received my Ph.D. in Computer Science and Engineering from the University of California, Santa Cruz, advised by Yang Liu, and was supported by the Chancellor’s Dissertation-Year Fellowship. In summer 2022 I was a student researcher at Google Brain, hosted by Ehsan Amid and Rohan Anil. Before that, I received an M.S. in Statistics from Stanford University (2019), advised by Johan Ugander, and a B.S. in Energy and Resources Engineering and B.A. in Economics from Peking University (2016).

Research

Leaderboards, drug-approval thresholds, and lending models all decide who gets ranked, approved, or rewarded, and the parties they evaluate adapt to them. I treat evaluation and decision-making as mechanisms: using game theory, mechanism design, statistics, and machine learning, I characterize how these rules shape behavior and design rules that prevent reward hacking and reward genuine improvement, under the constraints evaluators face in practice, such as imperfect verifiers and costly experiments. My work spans AI evaluation [1, 2], scientific discovery and regulation [3, 13], algorithmic recourse and lending [4, 6, 8], and fairness in deployed systems [9, 10, 11].

I’m especially excited about:

Incentive-aware AI evaluation

Benchmarks and leaderboards steer what model developers build, and developers in turn shape which benchmarks the field pays attention to. I want to understand this feedback loop and design evaluation protocols under which climbing a leaderboard requires real capability gains. For example, I study why benchmaxxing persists, and what alternative evaluation mechanisms can elicit model rankings that reflect true capabilities [1].

Incentive design for scientific discovery

Statistical tests act as contracts between those who produce evidence and those who act on it, such as regulators, journals, and funders. I study how significance thresholds and testing protocols determine which experiments get run, and how to design them so that evidence stays credible when firms and researchers respond strategically. For example, why might effective drugs for small patient populations, such as orphan drugs, never reach the market [3]?

Relevant work on strategic hypothesis testing and statistical contracts
  • [3] Strategic Hypothesis Testing. NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight
  • [13] The Price of Sample-Size-Agnostic Contracts. NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on E-Values: From Statistics to ML

Responsible decision-making under strategic adaptation

Algorithms increasingly decide who gets a loan, a job, or a public service, and the people affected respond to them. I design decision rules and learning algorithms that anticipate these responses and steer them toward genuine improvement rather than manipulation. Algorithmic recourse is one example: telling a rejected applicant what to change helps only if the institution has a reason to offer it and the advice leads to real improvement [4, 6, 8].

Aligning algorithmic decisions with societal values

A decision system is aligned only if it keeps serving the values it was designed for after deployment, as the populations it serves change and the people it affects respond. I study how fairness guarantees hold up over time and across populations, and how to keep fair decision rules practical to deploy [9, 10, 11].
